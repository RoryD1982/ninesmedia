Brand Identity in the Age of Scroll

Nines Media Team 8 April 2026 7 min read

Researchers studying mobile behaviour have found that the average person makes a scroll-stop decision in about 1.7 seconds. That's how long your brand has to register, communicate something meaningful, and earn the next moment of attention. Not the click. Just the pause.

This is the environment that modern brand identity has to survive in — not billboards, not glossy magazine spreads, but a phone screen moving at thumb-speed. The rules of good branding haven't changed entirely, but the hierarchy of what matters most has shifted dramatically. And businesses in South Africa and globally that haven't updated their visual identity systems for this reality are leaving recognition — and revenue — on the table.

The 1.7-Second Test

Before worrying about what your brand says about your values or aspirations, it needs to pass a simpler test: can someone identify it at a glance? Recognition precedes meaning. You cannot communicate what your brand stands for if the viewer doesn't register that it's yours.

The brands that consistently pass the 1.7-second test share a few characteristics:

  • A colour palette distinctive enough to be recognisable even when the logo is too small to read
  • A visual system — shapes, patterns, photography style, typographic treatment — that carries the brand beyond just the logo mark
  • Consistency across every touchpoint, so each exposure compounds the previous one
The strongest brand in any feed isn't necessarily the most beautiful one. It's the most consistent one. Every time it appears, it looks exactly like itself.

When we work on brand identity projects — whether for Johannesburg-based businesses or clients in Los Angeles and London — the first question we ask is: can someone who has seen your brand twice before identify a new piece of content as yours, without your logo visible? If the answer is no, the identity system needs work regardless of how attractive individual executions might be.

Why Consistency Beats Cleverness

There is a persistent temptation in brand design — particularly in the social media age — to chase novelty. A new visual trend emerges on Instagram, and businesses feel pressure to adopt it immediately to stay relevant. The result is a brand that looks different every six months, accumulating no recognition equity and starting from zero with every creative refresh.

Cleverness in branding has a place. A brand that never surprises becomes invisible through familiarity. But cleverness without consistency is just noise.

The framework that works: establish a distinctive, ownable visual system, and then be creative within it. The constraints of the system are what make the creativity feel branded rather than generic. Red Bull's visual world always feels like Red Bull even when the campaign concept changes entirely. Castle Lager has maintained brand recognition across generations of South Africans precisely because its visual cues have stayed stable even as executions have evolved.

Colour: Your Most Powerful Recognition Asset

Colour is processed faster than any other visual element. Before a viewer reads your name, registers your logo shape, or understands your message, they've already absorbed your colour. This makes your colour palette one of the most strategically important decisions in brand identity — and one of the most commonly underthought.

Most businesses arrive at their brand colour because it looked good in the initial logo design, or because it felt appropriate for the industry. That's a reasonable start, but it's not a complete approach. A brand colour needs to work across radically different contexts:

  • As a tiny circle in an Instagram grid
  • As a large background on a billboard
  • As a 32×32 pixel app icon
  • As a fill colour in a presentation template
  • Printed on merchandise
  • On a website at both light and dark mode

A colour that works beautifully as a gradient on a website hero image may become muddy and unrecognisable as a solid fill on a business card. Building a colour system — a primary colour, a secondary palette, and defined rules for how they're used together — is what prevents these inconsistencies.

In terms of the psychology behind colour choices, the shorthand guides (blue = trust, red = energy, green = nature) are not untrue, but they're oversimplified. The associations that matter most are the ones you build through consistent use over time, not the ones that theoretically come with the hue. A brand that uses an unexpected colour consistently will own that association in its category faster than a brand using the expected colour among ten competitors doing the same.

The Logo System: Designed for Every Surface

A single logo file is not a logo system. A complete logo system — which is what every brand operating across multiple platforms needs — includes:

  • Primary mark: The full logo with wordmark, used where space allows
  • Horizontal version: For website headers, email signatures, and document headers
  • Stacked version: For square or near-square applications
  • Icon or monogram: For small applications — app icons, favicon, social profile image, embossed packaging
  • Reversed versions: For use on dark or coloured backgrounds
  • Single-colour versions: For embroidery, single-colour printing, or watermark use

A logo that only exists in one horizontal version will be stretched, squeezed, or cropped every time it lands somewhere that doesn't accommodate that exact proportion — and it will be handled by whoever is making the asset at the time, which is almost never the brand designer. A proper logo system removes that decision from the equation and ensures the mark always appears correctly regardless of who is producing the content.

For South African businesses building regional or global reach — particularly those we work with expanding into the UK or Australian markets — this systematisation becomes even more important. When content is being produced across time zones by multiple people, brand guidelines with a complete logo system are what maintains visual cohesion.

Brand Voice: The Identity You Can Hear

Visual identity is the half of brand identity that most businesses invest in. Brand voice — how the brand sounds in written and spoken communication — is frequently underdeveloped or entirely undocumented, which means every piece of copy sounds slightly different depending on who wrote it.

Brand voice doesn't require a lengthy document. It requires clear decisions about a handful of dimensions:

  • Formality: Do you speak to your audience as peers, as a trusted expert, or as a service provider? Each implies a different register.
  • Energy: Is your tone calm and assured, or active and urgent? Does excitement show up in the copy or is the brand more measured?
  • Personality markers: What words, phrases, or cadences feel like your brand? What definitely doesn't?
  • Cultural context: For South African brands, decisions around language mixing — whether and how to incorporate local idiom — are genuine brand voice decisions with real impact on how the brand is perceived by different audiences.

A brand with a strong, consistent voice is recognisable in text even without visual cues. When someone reads your caption, email, or ad, they should feel the same personality they'd encounter on your website or in a conversation with your team.

Competing Globally from Johannesburg

One of the most significant shifts for South African brands in the past few years is that the digital environment has flattened the playing field. A brand based in Johannesburg competing for the attention of consumers in London or Sydney isn't disadvantaged by geography in the way it once was — but it is competing directly against brands with the full weight of UK and Australian creative industries behind them.

This raises the bar for visual identity quality. A brand that might read as sophisticated against local competition can feel underdeveloped in an international context. The standard to measure against isn't your domestic competitor — it's the best brand in your category globally.

The good news is that South African creative talent is genuinely world-class, and the cost base for developing strong brand identity work here is significantly more accessible than in London or LA. The opportunity is real. The gap that holds most SA brands back isn't creative talent — it's the willingness to invest in brand identity as infrastructure rather than treating it as a one-time cost to be minimised.

Brand Identity Is a Business Asset

Every impression your brand makes — every piece of content, every ad, every email — either builds or erodes recognition. A strong, consistent visual identity turns impressions into recognition, and recognition into preference. Recognition is what makes the person who saw your ad three weeks ago click through when they need what you sell.

In the age of scroll, where attention is genuinely the scarcest resource, brand identity is not decoration. It's infrastructure. It's what makes the 1.7 seconds count.
