What Makes a Website Work in 2026

By Nines Media Team 15 January 2026 8 min read

A website that loads in 6 seconds, falls apart on a phone screen, or makes visitors hunt for the contact form is not a neutral presence. It's actively costing you customers every day. The good news: the gap between a website that works and one that doesn't is increasingly predictable, and fixable.

Here's what separates high-performing websites in 2026 from the ones that just take up server space.

Page Speed Is Not Optional — Especially in South Africa

Core Web Vitals — Google's set of performance metrics measuring loading, interactivity, and visual stability — became a ranking factor a few years ago and remain one. But in South Africa, page speed has practical weight beyond SEO: a large portion of your visitors are on mobile networks, and a large portion of those are on capped data plans.

A visitor on a 4G connection in Johannesburg with 500MB of data left this month is making a split-second calculation when your page starts loading. If it doesn't respond quickly, they're gone — back to Google, likely to a competitor whose site loads faster.

The benchmarks Google targets:

  • Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) under 2.5 seconds — how quickly the main content appears
  • Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) under 0.1 — no elements jumping around as the page loads
  • Interaction to Next Paint (INP) under 200ms — the page responds to clicks and taps quickly

Most businesses don't know what their Core Web Vitals scores are. If you haven't checked yours in Google Search Console recently, that's the first thing to fix.

A page that renders in 1.8 seconds on a fibre connection can take 6–8 seconds on a typical SA mobile network if the images aren't properly optimised. That's the gap between a lead and a bounce.

Mobile-First Is the Starting Point, Not the Afterthought

The majority of web traffic in South Africa comes from mobile devices. If your website was designed on a desktop and "made responsive" afterwards, it almost certainly has mobile experience problems — text too small to read, buttons too close together to tap, images that don't scale, navigation that's fiddly on a phone.

Mobile-first design means you design the phone experience first, and expand from there. It forces decisions: what's the most important thing on this page? What does someone need to be able to do immediately? What can be deprioritised on a small screen?

A useful test: pull out your own phone, go to your website, and try to complete the primary action a customer would take — finding your contact details, requesting a quote, making a purchase. Note every moment of friction. That's your punch list.

A Clear Value Proposition Above the Fold

The "above the fold" area — what's visible on screen before any scrolling — is where most visitors decide whether to stay or leave. You have roughly three to five seconds to communicate three things: what you do, who you do it for, and why it's worth their time.

Most business websites fail this test. They open with a vague slogan ("Your success, our passion"), a generic stock photo, and a navigation bar. There's no reason to stay.

A strong above-the-fold section answers the visitor's first question — "Am I in the right place?" — immediately. It names the service, the customer, and the outcome. It's specific enough to feel like it was written for the person reading it.

Trust Signals That Actually Work

Before a visitor takes action on your site, they're making an unconscious trust assessment. The signals that move that needle aren't always the ones businesses prioritise.

  • Client logos — real logos of real clients, with permission (not stock-photo placeholders)
  • Verifiable testimonials — names, photos, company names; not generic "great service!" quotes
  • Certifications and accreditations — industry bodies, Google Partner badge, professional memberships
  • Team photos — real people, not stock imagery; SA audiences are particularly attuned to authentic vs. generic
  • A physical address — even if you work remotely, a listed address signals permanence
  • Case studies with real numbers — "we increased their leads by 40%" beats "we deliver results"

One Primary CTA Per Page

Decision fatigue is real. When a page has five different calls to action — download this, book a call, sign up for the newsletter, view our services, get a quote — the result is often that the visitor does none of them.

Every page on your site should have one primary action you want visitors to take. Secondary actions exist, but they're visually subordinate. The primary CTA appears multiple times — above the fold, mid-page, and at the bottom — but it's always the same ask.

What that CTA is matters too. "Get a Quote" works better than "Contact Us" for service businesses. "Book a Free Consultation" works better than "Schedule a Meeting." The more specific and lower-friction the action sounds, the higher the conversion rate.

Contact Forms vs. WhatsApp: Know Your Audience

This is a South Africa-specific consideration that surprises international clients we work with. In SA, WhatsApp is the preferred communication channel for a large proportion of the population. Many people will not fill in a contact form — they'll abandon the page — but they'll happily tap a WhatsApp button.

This doesn't mean ditching your contact form. It means offering both. A floating WhatsApp button, clearly visible on every page, captures the audience who wants a conversation before they're ready to commit to a formal inquiry. The form captures the more methodical buyer who wants to provide context upfront.

Businesses that add a WhatsApp button consistently see an increase in inquiries from that channel — often significant, often from customers who would not have contacted them otherwise.

SEO Baked In, Not Bolted On

Many businesses think about SEO as something that happens after the website is built. In practice, the structural decisions made during a website build determine how rankable the site is — and retrofitting good SEO architecture onto a poorly-structured site is painful and expensive.

The basics that need to be in place from the start:

  • Logical URL structure that reflects site hierarchy
  • Properly nested heading structure (one H1 per page, logical H2/H3 hierarchy)
  • Meta titles and descriptions planned as part of the build, not as an afterthought
  • Image alt text and file naming that describes content
  • Internal linking structure that distributes authority through the site
  • Schema markup for business type, reviews, and services where applicable
  • A sitemap submitted to Google Search Console

Why WordPress Isn't Always the Answer

WordPress powers a huge proportion of the web, and it's a legitimate choice for many use cases — particularly content-heavy sites or businesses that need a non-technical team to update content frequently. But it's not a default, and it's not always the best tool for the job.

The problems we see most often with WordPress builds in South Africa:

  • Plugin bloat — too many plugins, often conflicting, each adding load time and potential vulnerabilities
  • Themes that look polished in demos and perform poorly in practice
  • Page builders (particularly older versions of Divi and Elementor) that generate heavy, slow code
  • Maintenance overhead — WordPress requires ongoing updates, security monitoring, and backups

For many small and medium SA businesses, a lean custom-built or template-based static site will outperform a WordPress build on every metric that matters: speed, security, uptime, and cost of ownership. The question to ask is what the site actually needs to do — not what platform everyone else seems to be using.

Building for Fast and Slow Connections

We build sites for SA clients and for international clients in Los Angeles, London, Australia, and New Zealand. One principle applies everywhere, but matters most in South Africa: the site should work on a slow connection, not just a fast one.

That means compressed images (WebP format, lazy-loaded below the fold), minimal blocking scripts, efficient CSS, and no third-party widgets that make external requests on page load. The result is a site that loads quickly on fibre and acceptably on 4G — and one that ranks better and converts more visitors as a direct consequence.
